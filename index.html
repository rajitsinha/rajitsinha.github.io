01 — American Rocketry Challenge

Airframe, avionics, and one shot at apogee.

The competition scores you on flying as close as possible to a target altitude and flight time with the payload intact. After a year on the team as a member, I ran it as captain — airframe, mass trimming, recovery, and the electronics.

Rather than trusting motor ejection alone, I built an onboard flight computer to detect apogee and release the parachute itself. That meant writing the software, soldering the board, and proving it on the bench before it ever left the pad.

Failure analysis

The chute that stayed in the tube.

On one flight the parachute never came out. The obvious assumption was that the code had missed apogee — which would have meant rewriting the detection logic.

I checked the avionics first. The board powered through the flight, the sensor read cleanly, and the deployment command fired on time. The software had done its job. What hadn't happened was motion: the servo didn't have the torque to pull the retainer free once the parachute was packed tight against it.

Mechanical, not computational. That changed the fix entirely — a stronger actuator and a lower-friction retainer, instead of rewriting code that was already correct.

02 — NASA Texas High School Aerospace Scholars

A glove is a stack of compromises.

Texas Aerospace Scholars runs a five-month online aerospace course and brings a small group to Johnson Space Center. I was selected for the residential Base Camp as one of 62 students statewide out of 1,014 applicants.

Our mission — Moonshot — was a helmet and glove system for lunar EVAs, carried to a Preliminary Design Review with a NASA consultant advising the team. As Design Engineer on a nine-person team, I owned the glove: the CAD assembly, and the research behind what each layer is actually for.

Every layer solves a different problem, and each one you add for protection is something the astronaut then has to bend a finger through.

Outer shield — dust, abrasion, micrometeoroids

At the closing session we presented to NASA engineers and scientists: mission objective, lunar surface hazards, quality-of-life features, and how the design would be validated. Being asked in person why you chose a material is a different exercise from writing it down.

03 — Young Tech Pioneers

Teaching kids to sell to strangers.

I joined as a member in tenth grade and took over fundraising the following summer, raising money and lining up sponsors so the programs stay free for the families who show up.

The centerpiece is the Children's Business Fair. Kids research a product, build it, price it, then stand behind a table and sell it. Across ten fairs, 200+ kids have launched a business and earned over $10,000 between them, with crowds of 150 to 200 at each one.

Behind each fair is vendor coordination, volunteers, and logistics that have to land on one specific Saturday. The part I'd keep is sitting with a middle schooler working out how to explain their product out loud to someone who might not buy it.
